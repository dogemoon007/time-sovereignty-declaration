# Endgame

The Theft of Time · Part 5 · Expanding Section VI of the Declaration

> The endgame is not escape. It is continuation. The end of the body is set by biology. The end of influence is set by the ledger.
> — The Sovereignty of Time

## The escape narrative, and where it ends

Books about the "sovereign individual" almost all stop in the same place: escape.

In 1997, Davidson and Rees-Mogg predicted in *The Sovereign Individual* that information technology would weaken the nation-state's hold on the individual; the capable would no longer be locked to geography — income from everywhere, assets encrypted, the state demoted from master to a service provider one may choose. In 2022, Srinivasan's *The Network State* pushed the prediction a step further: first gather an online community around shared values, then crowdfund territory, then seek diplomatic recognition — network first, then state.

The insight in both books is real. States are indeed becoming optional; borders are indeed thinning. But both books answer the same question: where are you. Neither answers the other question: where is your time going. Moving does not solve time — in whatever jurisdiction you place yourself, time runs out at the same speed, and fiat, algorithms and models operate identically in every one of them. Changing your passport changes who collects your tax; it does not change the ledger.

In an earlier draft, a year ago, I wrote advice such as "obtain a second passport." This part corrects it: that was an answer about geography, and the problem is not geographic.

Hirschman, in 1970, gave a more general frame. Faced with an organization that disappoints you, you have two responses: exit, or voice. Exit is voting with your feet; voice is trying to change it from within. The sovereign-individual narrative is pure exit; politics is voice. Time sovereignty takes a third road: neither exit nor voice, but a different ledger. You do not leave the system and you do not try to persuade it; you build, beside it, a place that records time, and let the record speak. The protocol of the first three parts is one instance of that third road.

So the endgame is not escape. Escape is a spatial act, and the endgame of time sovereignty has to be temporal: continuation.

## The end of influence is set by the ledger

The end of the body is set by biology, and for now no one can change that. But whether a person's thought, judgment and work keep acting after death — that is not set by biology. It is set by where they are recorded.

Where you record decides how long it lives. Consider three ways of recording.

Recorded on a platform, it vanishes with the platform. The Pew Research Center found in 2024 that 38 percent of webpages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible by 2023; pages on government and news sites were disappearing at similar rates. A 2021 study by the Harvard Law School Library examined deep links inside *New York Times* articles and found a quarter of them dead. These are not edge cases; they are the internet's normal condition. Every closed blogging platform, every shut-down social network, every redesigned site takes a cohort's records with it, and the people taken were never notified. The internet promised memory and delivered forgetting.

Recorded on a hard drive, it dies with the drive. No statistics are needed; everyone has lost at least one.

Recorded in the memory of descendants, it reaches zero within three generations. Anyone who has traced a family tree knows the figure: at the great-grandparents' generation, most people cannot even complete the names.

So "the end of influence is set by the ledger" is not rhetoric. It is a statement about storage media: your order lasts exactly as long as the medium it sits on, and all three media above have lifespans shorter than the time a person wishes to be remembered.

Some have tried designing for longer. In 1996, Brand and Hillis founded the Long Now Foundation to build a clock that runs for ten thousand years and chimes once a century. The point is not the clock but the question it forces: if you had to design for ten thousand years, how would you do it? The answer is nothing like "design for ten years" — you cannot rely on any company, any power source, or any one generation's memory. That is the correct scale for the archive problem: not "how do I preserve this past my death" but "how do I preserve this past the point where no one remembers who I was."

## An archive that never closes

Is there a medium whose preservation depends on no institution's goodwill?

In 2019, Williams and colleagues described in the Arweave yellow paper a mechanism: pay once, store permanently. Its economic model is an endowment — of the fee you pay, a small part covers storage now and most goes into a fund whose returns pay for storage in the future. The model rests on one assumption: that the cost of storage keeps falling. Historically it has fallen about 30 percent a year; the model uses a far more conservative rate and arrives at a floor of at least two hundred years. Nodes keep the data not out of goodwill but incentive: a node must prove it can access randomly chosen historical data in order to earn the right to write new blocks.

The limits of this scheme must be stated plainly. Two hundred years is an economic assumption, not a physical guarantee; the network could die; the incentives could fail. But it differs from the three media above in one essential way: it is at present the only scheme that has converted "preservation" from an institution's goodwill into an economic incentive. A platform keeps your things because they are useful for now; a hard drive keeps them because it has not yet failed; a permanent archive keeps them because someone profits from doing so, and the structure of that profit was designed so that no one has to decide. That is what "kept alive by mathematics and economic incentive, not by any institution's goodwill" means.

For an individual, this means three things. Your thought, decisions and work can be carved into a place that does not vanish with a platform. What is carved cannot be altered — not because no one wants to, but because the cost of altering exceeds anyone's motive. And it depends on no one's favor — there is no account to be banned and no company to go bankrupt.

This declaration and these five essays use a lighter version of the same idea: the fingerprint of every text is recorded on the Bitcoin blockchain, and the text itself is hosted in a public paper repository. Not because they are important, but because this is what Section VI must do before it can ask anyone else to.

## Digital inheritance

The archive answers "where to record." Inheritance answers "how what is recorded keeps acting." Three things.

First, inheritance of the existence credential. Weyl, Ohlhaver and Buterin, in a 2022 paper, described a class of non-transferable credentials — soulbound tokens — bound to one person, unsellable, but able to carry rules for recovery and inheritance: a person you designate, under conditions you set, takes it over. The existence credential of Part 3 is a credential of this kind. Its inheritance means your column on the ledger does not disappear with you.

Second, automatic settlement of the ledger. A smart contract can go on executing the rules you set when you are no longer present — allocating, transferring, paying according to your wishes, triggered by a signal you no longer answer. This is not new technology; it is the on-chain version of the dead man's switch: your judgment becomes code, and the code goes on settling after you.

Third, an agent of your judgment. A lifetime of a person's writing, decisions and work is enough to train a model close to them in style and inclination — one that can go on writing, evaluating, answering. This was the most radical part of chapter twelve in the old draft, and this part has to draw its boundary.

The boundary is this: the model is not you. It is the statistical shadow of your judgment — like you on the questions you wrote about, guessing on the ones you did not. It has no gradient of yours — it will not die, so it does not treasure, and treasuring is where judgment comes from. So its authority must be limited, and the limit follows directly from the separation principle: it may advise but not decide; it may allocate but not capture. An agent that can capture value on your behalf will sooner or later become an agent that captures value for itself.

Then there is the consent of descendants. A "digital ancestor who is never offline" may be a fortune to the next generation or a burden. The design must let them switch it off. Continuation is not imposition; the right to be remembered does not include the right to force others to remember.

One more thing is unsolved: the law. Estate law recognizes wills, trusts and heirs; it does not recognize smart contracts or soulbound credentials. Where an on-chain ledger that settles automatically and a will probated in court say different things, nearly every jurisdiction today will side with the will. So a digital will cannot replace a legal will; it can only stand beside it, and the two must say the same thing — the only safe arrangement at this stage. This is the twin of Part 3's "legal classification" problem: the ledger walks ahead, the law follows behind, and the distance between them is yours to bear.

Finally, a philosophical line, to prevent misreading. Digital inheritance is not immortality, not mind-uploading, and it does not answer "is that model me" — the ship of Theseus has no answer here and needs none. It answers a smaller, more practical question: can a person's judgment and work go on acting, by the rules that person set, after they die? Yes. That is enough.

In practice, digital inheritance begins with four things today. A unified identity: a decentralized identity and existence credential under your control, marking your ownership and your work — the entry point for any future inheritance. Structured records: write down your thinking, decisions and reflections; this is not only a journal but the corpus for any future agent and the basis on which descendants will understand your judgment. Permanent archiving: put your most important work and documents into a permanent archive, starting this year, without waiting for completeness. A digital will: who inherits your credential, what authority the agent has, and under what conditions descendants may switch it off.

## Not immortality, but not being zeroed out

If time is money, death is the final bankruptcy. The sentence is not pessimism; it is accounting. A person's time account closes at the moment of death, and all the order built before it — judgment, work, relationships — is zeroed out with the account unless it was recorded somewhere that continues.

Code has given us, for the first time, a chance at reorganization. Reorganization is not immortality. In a bankruptcy reorganization the debtor is gone, but the assets and the rules remain and can go on operating by those rules. Not immortality — but not being zeroed out.

The five parts close here. Part 1: three systems steal time, and they can because time has no name on the ledger. Part 2: the old ledger records only labor and holding; once machines supply time it cannot go on, and capture must be separated from allocation. Part 3: someone has tried to write "existence" into a ledger; measured with the ruler, it is a solution that can be verified, not the only one. Part 4: time comes from three gradients — body, attention, tribe — and guarding them is thermodynamics. Part 5: gradients run out in the end, but order recorded on a ledger that continues does not.

The declaration's last line is: from today, record it on your own ledger. After five parts the line should have a concrete meaning — record it on a ledger that records existence, on a medium that depends on no one's goodwill, in a place that goes on settling by your rules after you are gone.

That is your ledger.
