# Three Thefts

The Theft of Time · Part 1 · Expanding Section II of the Declaration

> Three systems, one after another, learned how to steal time. The three have one thing in common: none of them asked you.
> — The Sovereignty of Time

## Why "theft"

The word is heavy, so it needs defining first.

Robbery is overt. Someone takes what is yours in front of you; you know what happened, you can resist, and the law is on your side. Fraud is delayed. You agreed at the time, then found the premise was false, and you can seek redress. Theft, in the sense used here, is neither. It happens while you are not in the room, inside the default settings. You do nothing, and it is done; you notice nothing, because it was designed from the start not to be noticed.

I use "theft" rather than a politer word because every politer word hands the responsibility back to you. "Inflation is part of the cycle" — so you should learn to invest. "Scrolling is a self-control problem" — so you should be more disciplined. "Being replaced by AI means you stopped learning" — so you should reskill. The three sentences have the same structure: the system takes something from you, then tells you it is your fault.

"Theft" puts the responsibility back on the system. The definition is specific: transfer without consent. The three sections below take one system each — how it performs the transfer, which part of your time it takes, and why you were never asked.

## I. Fiat steals your past

Start with what savings are.

Savings are not money. Savings are the balance of time you have already spent. In some year, on some few hundred early mornings and late nights, you did things other people needed and received a number in return; the number stands for those mornings and nights, which are gone and cannot be had again. Savings are in the past tense. They are the part of your life that has been spent and not yet redeemed.

On 15 August 1971 the United States closed the window at which dollars could be exchanged for gold. From that day, the quantity of the world's major currencies was no longer bound by anything physical, only by policy. The significance is not gold itself — gold was merely the carrier of a constraint — but what the system gained once the constraint was gone: the freedom to issue.

How does issuance steal? It never touches the number in your account. Your hundred is still a hundred. What changes is how many hours of other people's labor that hundred can buy back. By the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' consumer price index, 100 dollars in 1971 corresponds to roughly 832 dollars in 2026 — which is to say that a person who put away 100 dollars in cash in 1971 and held it until today holds about 12 percent of the purchasing power they started with. An average of 3.9 percent a year sounds mild; after fifty-five years, an eighth is left. Every fiat currency does this; they differ only in speed.

Economics has a word for it: seigniorage. The issuer creates new money at almost no cost and uses it to buy real goods and labor. The tax base is everyone holding the currency, the rate is the pace of issuance, and the method of collection is dilution. It appears on no tax return.

The tax has a second property: it is regressive. New money does not fall evenly on everyone. It enters at the point of issue and moves outward — through banks, governments, holders of large assets — reaching wages and cash savings last. Those who get it first spend at old prices; those who get it last spend at new ones. This is the Cantillon effect. Asset holders are compensated for inflation because assets rise with the money supply; people who live on wages and cash savings are not, because wages are always the last to rise. Seigniorage is therefore not only a tax without a return; it is a tax paid mostly by the patient and the asset-less.

There is a subtler layer. Irving Fisher, in 1930, explained interest as the reward for waiting: you postpone consumption, you let others use the resources first, and interest compensates your patience. When the real rate is negative — when issuance outruns interest — waiting is no longer compensated but punished. A system that persistently punishes patience and rewards immediate consumption and debt is stealing more than the purchasing power of savings. It is stealing the meaning of saving. It teaches, systematically, not to set anything aside for the future.

The counterargument deserves a hearing: moderate inflation is necessary, and deflation is worse — falling prices make people delay purchases, revenues fall, layoffs follow, prices fall further. At the macro level the argument has merit, and I will not rebut it here. But it answers a different question. It answers "what the system needs," not "who pays." Even if moderate inflation is optimal for the system as a whole, that optimum is achieved through a tax that no one consented to, no one sees, and savers mostly pay. A necessary tax is still a tax, and a tax without a return still needs someone to ask: who agreed?

No one asked you. A saver's "consent" is presumed from the act of holding the currency, and you were born into a fiat jurisdiction — holding it was not a choice but a starting point. Your only exit is to exchange it for something else, and what to exchange it for, when, and what happens if you are wrong, are costs and risks borne entirely by you. Staying requires no action; leaving requires knowledge, judgment and luck. That is the power of the default.

One more thing happened in 1971: Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen published *The Entropy Law and the Economic Process*, arguing that economic activity is fundamentally an entropic process — low-entropy resources in, high-entropy waste out, irreversibly. That connects to the deeper frame of this series and is taken up later. But the coincidence of dates is not nothing. People of that era already sensed that once a constraint is removed, some things only flow one way.

## II. Algorithms steal your present

Also in 1971, Herbert Simon wrote a sentence that has been quoted ever since: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. His point was about design — since attention is scarce, information systems should be designed to conserve it. Fifty years later, information systems are designed for the opposite purpose: to consume as much of it as possible.

What is attention? It is what time looks like right now. Whatever you are looking at is what you are living in. The past has been spent, the future has not arrived, and the only thing you truly possess is the second passing now — and the content of that second is wherever your attention is. To steal attention is not to steal some portion of your time. It is to steal the only tense in which you are alive.

How is it stolen? By auction.

The core mechanism of internet advertising is a real-time auction. In 2007, Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz described in the *American Economic Review* the "generalized second-price auction" used by Google and Yahoo: every ad slot is auctioned at every impression; the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest price. The mechanisms have grown more complex since, but the skeleton stands. Every time you refresh a page, an auction completes in tens of milliseconds, and what is being sold is "this person's attention for the next few seconds."

Look at who is in the room. The buyers are advertisers; the seller is the platform; the auctioneer is the algorithm. What are you? You are the lot. The lot does not set the price, does not share the proceeds, and does not know the auction took place.

This makes the recommendation engine's role clear. Its objective function is not "find this person the best content" but "keep this person here as long as possible," because time on site determines the number of auctions. It is not finding content for you; it is finding you for advertisers. The algorithm seems to understand you because understanding you is the shortest path to keeping you.

So no detail of the product is neutral. Infinite scroll removes "reaching the bottom," a natural stopping cue. Autoplay removes "pressing next," a decision. Red badges and vibrations convert an action you used to initiate — "let me see if there's anything new" — into one initiated by the device. None of these designs exists to help you decide when to stop; all of them exist to remove "stop" as an available option. The length of time a person is auctioned equals the length of time they cannot find the stop button.

The cost is measurable. In 2008, Gloria Mark and colleagues at the University of California, Irvine, interrupted people at work and measured how long it took them to return to the original task: about twenty-three minutes on average. Her second finding matters more: people compensate for interruptions by working faster, at the cost of more stress and frustration. Fragmentation does not steal only the minutes of the interruption; it steals the twenty-three minutes afterward, and the extra toll you pay trying to win them back.

The counterargument: the service is free, so this is an exchange, not a theft. It fails twice. First, the price is not zero — you pay in the only non-renewable thing you own; the bill simply omits that line. Second, an exchange requires both sides to price, and here only one does: the platform decides what an hour of your attention is worth in advertising, and you neither know the figure nor can negotiate it. An exchange in which only one party knows the price is not called an exchange in any market.

No one asked you. What you "agreed" to is a terms-of-service document no one reads, and the default outcome of agreeing is that your attention is auctioned, the proceeds go to the platform, and you receive free content — which is to say, you think you are consuming, and in fact you are being consumed. Leaving requires uninstalling, self-restraint, and the social cost of absence. Staying requires nothing.

## III. AI steals your future

What is expertise? It is your pricing power over the time you have left.

A person spends ten years learning a profession not for those ten years but for the thirty that follow, in which an hour of their time sells for more than someone else's. That price is their valuation of their own future hours, and its basis is: others cannot do this, or cannot do it as well. Expertise is a contract about the future, bought with ten years of the past.

Now look at what happened to the other party to that contract.

In November 2024, an analysis by a16z tracked the price of large language model inference: for models of equivalent capability, the cost per million tokens fell from 60 dollars to 0.06 dollars over three years — a factor of a thousand, or roughly tenfold a year. That pace exceeds the decline of computing costs in the personal-computer era. The specific numbers change monthly; the direction does not. The marginal cost of cognitive labor is heading toward zero.

Goldman Sachs estimated in March 2023 that generative AI could expose the equivalent of 300 million full-time jobs to automation, with office and administrative work most affected — precisely the jobs priced on expertise.

But unemployment is not the heart of this. Most people will not be told one day that they have been replaced. What happens is quieter: your hour gets repriced. The new basis is no longer "others cannot do this" but "what does it cost a machine to do this." The future contract you bought with ten years of your past has been unilaterally revalued, and the one doing the valuing is not you.

The counterargument is historical: every technological revolution destroyed some jobs and created others — steam, electricity, computing — and this one will be no exception. I hope that is right, but one structural difference cannot be skipped. Every previous substitution replaced what humans sold cheaply — muscle, repetition, memory — and pushed people toward what sold dearly: judgment, creativity, expertise. Every previous substitution raised the price of cognition. This one substitutes cognition itself. The mechanism "pushed toward something dearer" requires that something dearer exists; this time, we have not yet seen where that next rung is. Perhaps it will appear. Until it does, the people being repriced have nowhere to go, and the repricing has already happened.

This is the theft of the future. Fiat takes the time you have already spent; algorithms take the time you are spending; AI takes the time you have not yet spent but have already priced.

There is a deeper layer still. These models are trained on human output — articles, code, conversations, work — including yours. Your output is taken first to train the model, and the trained model then returns to price you. Two transfers, neither with your consent. The first takes your past output; the second takes your future pricing power. And "opting out," as with the other two thefts, is an option you must discover, apply for, and prove — the default is in.

This is not a moral accusation. It is a structural description: at every link in this chain, your time is not a variable.

## IV. None of them asked you

Put the three together and the structure is identical.

Each theft is a default. Staying requires no action: you are born into fiat, you open your phone into the algorithm, your output is in the training data the moment it is published. Leaving each one requires an action, and the cost of that action — knowledge, time, risk, social price — is borne entirely by you.

Each theft hands responsibility back to you. Inflation teaches you to invest, the algorithm teaches you self-discipline, AI teaches you lifelong learning. All three are true, and none is the point. The point is that in the objective function of each of the three systems, your time is not a term. The central bank's target is prices and employment; the platform's is time on site; the model company's is capability and cost. No one wants to steal your time. It simply is not on anyone's ledger.

So what would consent look like? Three tests, each of them plain:

First, you can see it. When the transfer happens, you can see the amount, the time, and the destination. Seigniorage has no return; the auction does not notify the lot; training does not notify the author. All three fail.

Second, you can say no, and saying no costs nothing. An option that requires money, time or risk to refuse is not an option; it is a tollbooth. Changing currency, quitting the phone, applying to opt out of training — all three charge a toll.

Third, your time is a variable on the ledger. Only when a transfer is recorded on a ledger denominated in your time can "consent" even be defined — otherwise you cannot say what you would be consenting to. On the ledgers of all three systems, that entry does not exist.

The three thefts are not three bad acts by three bad actors. They are three expressions of the same gap: our ledgers record "who holds how much" and "who did the work," and not "who exists" or "whose time." Money can be issued without limit, attention can be auctioned without paying its owner, and cognition can be copied without paying its author, because of one shared precondition — time has no name on the ledger.

So the question is not "how do we resist." Resisting a system that does not treat you as a variable is like protesting to a machine: it cannot hear you, not out of malice, but because it has no input port for that.

The question is: what should the ledger record?

That is the subject of Part 2. When machines begin to supply time, labor ceases to be the basis of distribution — and on the old ledger, besides labor, there is nothing.

---

Fiat steals your past, algorithms steal your present, AI steals your future. Time sovereignty is taking all three back.
