# Distribution

The Theft of Time · Part 2 · Expanding Section IV of the Declaration

> The old mechanism of distribution was labor: you put in time, you got back a share. When machines put in the time, the chain breaks.
> — The Sovereignty of Time

## A ten-thousand-year chain

Part 1 ended with a conclusion: the three thefts are possible because time has no name on the ledger. This part is about what the ledger does record, why it is about to stop working, and why changing the currency will not save it.

Humanity has had only one large-scale mechanism of distribution: labor. You put in time, you got back a share. Among hunter-gatherers, sharing was the norm, but the basis of sharing was still who had contributed. Agriculture fixed the chain in place — land plus labor, for ten thousand years. Industry refined it to its barest form, the hourly wage: what you sell is not a product but hours. The chain has one remarkable virtue: it is legible. It is not fair — capital, land and birth keep separate accounts outside it — but how much a person holds is, roughly, a function of how much time they have put in. Every struggle over fairness in the last two centuries — the eight-hour day, the minimum wage, pensions, unions, labor law — has been patchwork on this chain, never an attempt to replace it.

In 1930, Keynes wrote a short essay, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." He predicted that within a century technological progress would solve the problem of production, that people would need to work only fifteen hours a week, and that the remaining difficulty would be what to do with leisure. He saw half of it. Production is indeed being solved — output per person has multiplied several times since he wrote. But the fifteen-hour week never arrived, and the reason is not technology but the ledger: shares are tied to labor, no work means no share, so no one dares work less. Keynes assumed distribution would follow production automatically. It did not, because the chain itself needed replacing, and he did not see that.

The chain was fraying before AI. Karabarbounis and Neiman, in 2014, measured the global labor share — the fraction of national income going to labor — and found it had fallen by about five percentage points since the early 1980s, across countries and industries. Another set of figures is more vivid: the Economic Policy Institute's long-running "productivity–pay gap" shows US labor productivity rising about 80 percent since 1979 while the hourly compensation of the typical worker rose about 30 percent — for the thirty years before that, the two lines had moved together. Output and share parted ways in the late 1970s, a few years after the monetary constraint described in Part 1 was removed. Capital has been quietly substituting for labor for four decades. AI is not the start of the trend; it is the accelerator.

## When machines put in the time

A machine can supply ten thousand hours of cognitive labor in a second, and none of those hours belongs to anyone's life. That sentence contains something that has never existed before: time without a life behind it.

The labor chain works because time comes out of a life — behind every hour of labor there is a person who lived that hour once and will not live it again, and the share compensates that expenditure. When time can come from somewhere other than a life, the object of compensation disappears. The machine puts in the time; who gets the share? By the logic of the labor chain, the machine's owner, because he "put in" the machine. But what he put in was capital, not time — and the capital chain was never a distribution chain. It is a concentration chain: returns to capital go to capital, by definition.

Korinek and Stiglitz, in a 2017 paper, put the matter plainly: the effect of AI on income distribution is determined not by the technology but by institutions; under present institutions, the likely outcome is a further decline in the labor share and wages pushed toward the cost of machines. They propose various redistributive tools, but every tool is a transfer on the old ledger — let the chain break, then use taxes to carry some share back.

Harari calls the people thrown off this chain the "useless class." The phrase is accurate and cruel, and the way it is accurate deserves attention: it looks at people from the standpoint of the labor chain. A person with no entry on the labor ledger is "useless." Switch ledgers and the same person is not useless; they exist. "Useless" is not a judgment about the person but about the ledger — it says that this book accepts only one kind of input.

So a combination appears that has never existed: unlimited output, and people with nowhere to go. Content can be generated without limit at zero marginal cost; routine cognitive services — most of law, accounting, programming, translation — are becoming a utility. Production is no longer scarce. Distribution is: who gets these outputs, and on what basis. And our ledgers record only two things — who holds how much (capital) and who did the work (labor). Capital is concentrating, labor is depreciating, and there is no third column.

This is not a technical problem. Technology has solved production; it has solved it too well. It is a ledger problem: when labor stops being the basis of distribution, the ledger must find another basis, and technology will not do that on its own.

Some will say: then let everyone own the machines. Employee ownership, universal basic capital, sovereign wealth funds — Norway's oil fund, owned by all its citizens, is the ready example. This is better than transfers, because it reaches the source of capture. But notice what it changes: it admits more people into the "who holds how much" column; the basis of distribution is still holding. Holding concentrates — that is the nature of capital, not a moral flaw — so distribution by holding returns, over time, to where it started, unless holding is bound to something that does not concentrate. What does not concentrate? A person can exist only once. That is why the basis must move to existence, not to "more people holding."

Ledgers have not entirely ignored existence. Censuses record it; citizenship records it; the basic-income experiments of the past decade — Finland's two-thousand-person trial in 2017–18, Stockton's one-hundred-and-twenty-five-person trial from 2019, GiveDirectly's long-running trial in Kenya — all distribute shares by existence rather than labor, and their results broadly agree: incomes stabilized, employment did not fall, mental health improved. They show that distribution by existence works at the level of the person. Their limit is on the other side: where the money comes from. Every one of them was funded by a capturer — a government or a foundation — so they are transfers, not mechanisms; when the funder stops, the share stops. Which raises the question that follows: if the basis is to be existence, the money must come directly from the source of capture, not pass through the capturer's hands.

## Why changing the currency is not enough

By this point many readers will have a ready answer: change the currency. Over the past fifteen years three attempts have appeared, each worth taking seriously, each insufficient.

Bitcoin changed who may issue money. Nakamoto's 2008 paper gave the world a currency with fixed supply that no one can inflate — the most direct response to the first theft: savings can no longer be diluted. It did what it promised. But look at what it changed and what it did not. It replaced the unit on the ledger; what the ledger records is unchanged — still "who holds how much." Bitcoin's basis of distribution is hashpower and early arrival, a mixture of the capital chain and timing, not a new basis. It is a better savings technology, not a different distribution.

Stablecoins simply moved fiat itself onto the chain. By 2026 the market exceeds three hundred billion dollars. It proves the efficiency of on-chain settlement, and it proves something more interesting: when people are free to choose their on-chain unit, most choose the dollar. Stablecoins are a victory for efficiency and a surrender of sovereignty — they did not even change the unit.

Memecoins removed the barrier to issuing money. Anyone can create a currency in minutes, which democratizes "the right to issue" to the point of absurdity: millions of micro-central-banks at once. The experiment has an unexpected pedagogical value — it proves that freedom to issue is not justice in distribution. Everyone can mint; the rules of distribution are exactly what they were a century ago: first in, first paid, fastest out wins.

The three share one property: as long as a currency's rise and fall is decided by capital flows, its value is still denominated in fiat and its holders are still playing a casino's game — zero-sum, volatile, attention-driven. The revolution is incomplete not for want of technology but because they changed the unit on the ledger without changing what the ledger records.

Bitcoin's believers will object: a savings technology does not need to solve distribution; it needs to do one thing well. The objection is correct, and it is precisely this series' argument — a savings technology addresses the first theft; it does not address the second or the third, nor distribution after AI. It is necessary, not sufficient. A ledger that can see only holders sees not people but balances; on such a ledger, not holding is the same as not existing.

## Capture and allocation must be separated

Now the central principle of this series can be stated. Two definitions first.

Capture is turning output into disposable value — who receives the revenue. Allocation is deciding whom the value belongs to. On the labor chain, the two are performed by a single act: a wage is both capture (the firm pays) and allocation (the worker receives); the market executes them together, so no one needs to tell them apart.

The machine age pulls them apart. Capture concentrates in the owners of machines — that is the nature of capital, and there is nothing wrong with it. The problem is allocation: if allocation is also decided by the machine owners, then everything the machines produce belongs by default to the machine owners, and everyone else receives what the owners choose to give. That is not distribution. It is enclosure. In sixteenth- to eighteenth-century England, the commons were enclosed as private property, and the people who had lived from them became landless laborers — Polanyi called this the origin of labor's commodification. The commons being enclosed today is cognition: models are trained on the output of all humanity, and the output belongs to those who trained the models.

So the principle is: the mechanism that captures value must not also be the mechanism that allocates it.

This is not a new intuition. It is the intuition behind humanity's most mature institutional designs. Montesquieu's separation of powers: those who make the law must not enforce it. Berle and Means' 1932 separation of ownership and control: those who fund and those who manage must not be one will. Central-bank independence: the department that spends must not be the department that prints. Auditor independence: those who keep the books must not audit them. Each says the same thing — the referee cannot also be a player.

The principle has a corollary that is easy to miss: the state is a capturer too. Taxation is capture. So "let the state allocate" does not automatically satisfy separation — it moves allocation from one capturer to another. True separation requires an allocation mechanism independent of every capturer, firms and states alike. That is why what follows is a discussion of protocols rather than policies: policy is a capturer's instrument; a protocol need not be.

Put this part together with the last, and you have a ruler. Any proposal claiming to solve "distribution after AI" can be measured on four marks:

First, is its basis of distribution independent of labor? If it still distributes by labor, it is patching a chain that has already broken.

Second, are capture and allocation separated? If the allocator is also a capturer — firm or state — it is a refinement of enclosure.

Third, does it pass the three tests of consent: the transfer is visible, saying no costs nothing, and time is a variable on the ledger?

Fourth, can it be verified? An unverifiable scheme of distribution is a promise, not a mechanism.

Part 3 is about an attempt someone has already made to write these four into a protocol. I will measure it with this ruler rather than speak on its behalf.
