## The Declaration

### I. Time

Time is the only non-renewable resource. Money can be earned again, information can be copied, energy can be converted. Time moves in one direction only. This is not rhetoric; it is physics. Entropy gives time its arrow, and you happen to live along it.

So the foundation of every sovereignty is sovereignty over time. Territorial sovereignty, monetary sovereignty, data sovereignty — each answers the same question: who gets to decide what happens during a given stretch of time. For an individual the question is simpler and harsher. Your life is a sum of time being spent, irreversibly, and you have never seen the ledger.

This declaration does one thing. It opens that ledger: where your time has been going, why you never agreed to it, and how to record it back under your own name.

### II. Three Thefts

Three systems, one after another, learned how to steal time.

By “steal” I mean something specific: transfer without consent. Not robbery — you would resist robbery. Not fraud — you would discover fraud afterwards. Theft, in this sense, happens inside the default settings. You do nothing, and it is done.

Fiat steals your past. Savings are the condensed form of time you have already spent: years of early mornings and late nights, exchanged for a number. That number stands for time, not paper. And the one real freedom a fiat system has is to issue more. Every issuance leaves the number in your account untouched and changes only how much time it can buy back. Inflation is not an economic phenomenon. It is the theft of time from savers — the gentlest possible way to take the least renewable thing you own.

Algorithms steal your present. Attention is what time looks like right now: whatever you are looking at is what you are living in. The business of a recommendation engine is to cut that time into pieces, auction them, and cut again. Every refresh is a bid, and what is being bid on is the next fifteen seconds of your life. You are not a party to the auction. You are the lot.

AI steals your future. Expertise used to be your pricing power over the time you have left: ten years of study is why your hour is worth what it is. When cognition can be copied without limit, and each copy costs almost nothing, that pricing power leaves your hands. This does not mean you will lose your job. It means your future hours will be priced by someone else — and priced cheaply.

The three have one thing in common: none of them asked you. You never signed an agreement letting a central bank dilute your savings, or an algorithm auction your attention, or a machine train on your output and then price you. Staying in requires no action. Leaving each one does. That is what theft means.

### III. Gradient

The iron law of the universe is that entropy increases. The sentence has been repeated so often that most people draw the wrong conclusion from it: since order is doomed to decay, every effort is a heroic, losing struggle.

That is not how life has ever survived. Life is an open system. It does not fight entropy; it consumes an energy gradient, exports its entropy, and buys internal order with the difference. The temperature gap between the sun and the earth feeds everything alive; the gap between a bowl of rice and a hungry body feeds you. Entropy is the universe’s tax. Order is what you buy with a gradient. No one is exempt from the tax, but everyone decides who gets their gradient.

So the question for a sovereign of time was never “how do I fight.” It is two more concrete questions: What is your gradient? And is someone else collecting it?

You have exactly three. Your body, where energy becomes time — its efficiency sets how many hours you have. Your attention, where time becomes order — its integrity sets what those hours can produce. Your tribe, where order compounds — one person’s order dies with them; a group’s order can be passed on. The three thefts take precisely these three gradients: fiat drains the reserves your body earned, algorithms shred your attention, and AI makes your expertise no longer enough to gather a tribe around.

Guarding your gradients is not self-improvement advice. It is thermodynamics.

### IV. Distribution

The old mechanism of distribution was labor: you put in time, you got back a share. That chain ran for ten thousand years. It was unfair, but it was legible — how much a person held was, roughly, a function of how much time they had put in. Wages, working hours, pensions, labor law: all of it is patchwork on that one chain.

When machines put in the time, the chain breaks. A machine can supply ten thousand hours of cognitive labor in a second, and none of those hours belong to anyone’s life. After AI, production is no longer scarce. Distribution is. Machines amplify every output, and no mechanism says who the amplified part belongs to — so by default it belongs to whoever owns the machine.

A civilization can therefore have unlimited output and nowhere to put its people. This is not a technical problem; the technology has already solved production. It is a ledger problem. Our ledgers only know how to record “who did the work.” They have no entry for “who exists.” When labor stops being the basis of distribution, the ledger has to find another one.

Here is a principle I take to be a hard constraint: the mechanism that captures value cannot also be the mechanism that distributes it. When the owner of the machine decides who gets the machine’s output, that is not distribution. It is enclosure. Capture and allocation must be separated. It is the ruler I hold against any proposed scheme of distribution.

### V. A Protocol

Changing the currency is not enough. Bitcoin changed who may issue money; memecoins removed the barrier to issuing it; stablecoins simply moved fiat itself onto the chain. Each succeeded on some axis, and none escaped fiat: as long as a currency’s rise and fall is decided by capital flows, its value is still denominated in fiat and its holders are still playing a casino’s game. The revolution is incomplete — not for lack of technology, but because they changed the unit on the ledger without changing what the ledger records. It still records “who holds how much,” not “who exists.”

Someone has already written a different idea into a protocol and has been testing it on several chains since 2023. I treat it as one of many possible schemes of distribution. Its design has three layers.

First, proof of existence in time. Existence itself is recorded as a credential that cannot be diluted. Existence is not identity, not an asset, not what you own or what you have done; it is the fact that you were here, in time. It is the hardest fact in the off-chain world, and the only one a machine cannot generate on your behalf. Put it on the ledger, and distribution has a basis that does not depend on labor. This layer can be implemented many ways; this protocol is one.

Second, a market for attention and creativity. On top of the existence credential, human attention and human creativity are priced — by the people giving them, not by the platform. This layer turns the present that algorithms stole into a present you can sell yourself.

Third, an AI production tax. Every output amplified by machines pays a tax to human time. Not to a government: to the holders of existence credentials — to everyone who has been here in time. The value a machine creates with ten thousand hours of cognitive labor flows back to the people the machine has priced.

It has run into the problems every protocol of this kind must face: how proof of existence resists fabricated existence, who sets the tax rate, how a market prices anything without liquidity. These are not all solved. It has also proven something possible: a basis for distribution that does not depend on labor can be written as code, used by a few hundred strangers, and stay alive across several chains for nearly four years.

It is not the only solution, and it may not be the best one. It is a solution that can be verified. I would rather see you propose your own protocol of distribution than believe in this one.

### VI. Endgame

The endgame is not escape. It is continuation.

The sovereign-individual story usually stops at escape: change your country, change your currency, change your identity. That mistakes sovereignty over time for freedom of movement. Moving does not solve time. Wherever you take yourself, it runs out at the same speed.

The end of the body is set by biology. The end of influence is set by the ledger. Whether a person’s thoughts, decisions and work keep acting after they die depends on where those things are recorded. Recorded on a platform, they vanish with the platform; on a hard drive, with the drive; in the memory of descendants, within three generations. The internet promised memory and delivered forgetting.

Carve them instead into an archive that never closes — one kept alive by mathematics and economic incentive, not by any institution’s goodwill. Let them go on being read, executed and inherited after you: your judgment trained into an agent that keeps judging in your place, your existence credential assigned to an heir, your ledger settling while you are no longer present.

If time is money, death is the final bankruptcy. Code has given us, for the first time, a chance at reorganization — not immortality, but not being zeroed out.

### VII. Choice

You cannot choose whether time runs out. Physics decided that for you.

You can only choose whose ledger it is recorded on. On the central bank’s ledger it is inflation. On the platform’s ledger it is traffic. On the machine owner’s ledger it is depreciation of compute. On your own ledger, it is your life.

This declaration asks you to believe nothing. It asks you to look at the ledger, once.

From today, record it on your own.
